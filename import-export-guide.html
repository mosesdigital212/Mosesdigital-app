Import & Export Business Guide for Beginners in Kenya

Importing and exporting can create opportunities for entrepreneurs, wholesalers, retailers and established businesses. However, international trade involves more than finding a cheap product and arranging delivery. It requires careful planning, supplier verification, cost calculation, documentation and an understanding of the rules that apply to the goods being traded.

This guide introduces the basic steps involved in starting an import or export activity in Kenya and highlights the decisions that should be made before money is committed.

1. Understand the Import and Export Business

Importing means purchasing goods from another country and bringing them into your country for use, resale or production. Exporting means selling goods from your country to customers in another country.

Importers may supply shops, factories, online customers, contractors or other businesses. Exporters may sell locally produced agricultural goods, manufactured products, crafts or other products to international buyers.

Before starting, identify exactly what product you want to trade, who will buy it and why customers would choose you instead of another seller.

2. Choose the Right Product

A product that looks profitable overseas may not be profitable after transport, taxes, handling, storage and local selling expenses are included.

When selecting a product, consider:

  • Demand in the Kenyan market or your target export market.
  • Competition and the selling prices of similar products.
  • Product quality and durability.
  • Size, weight and shipping requirements.
  • Whether the product requires permits, certification or special handling.
  • How quickly you expect to sell the stock.

Start with research. Speak to potential buyers, compare prices and avoid ordering large quantities based only on assumptions.

3. Find and Verify Your Supplier

Suppliers can be found through trade exhibitions, manufacturer websites, business directories, sourcing platforms, referrals and direct contact with manufacturers or distributors.

Before making payment, carry out checks such as:

  • Confirm the supplier's registered business name and contact details.
  • Request a detailed quotation showing product specifications and quantities.
  • Ask for product photographs, videos or samples where possible.
  • Check references, reviews and business history.
  • Confirm payment terms and the account receiving the funds.
  • Ask how defective, missing or delayed goods are handled.
Important: A professional website or attractive quotation does not prove that a supplier is genuine. Verify the business independently before sending money.

4. Calculate the Full Import Cost

The supplier's price is only one part of the total cost of importing. Your final cost may include freight, insurance, customs duties, taxes, clearing charges, port or terminal charges, transport and other expenses depending on the shipment.

Build your calculation using actual quotations and the requirements applicable to your product.

  • Product purchase price
  • International shipping or freight
  • Insurance, where applicable
  • Customs duty, if applicable
  • VAT and other applicable taxes
  • Import declaration and processing costs
  • Clearing agent fees
  • Port, terminal, storage or handling costs
  • Transport from the arrival point to your premises
  • Bank, currency conversion or payment fees

Divide the total landed cost by the number of saleable units to estimate the cost per unit. This helps you set a realistic selling price and evaluate your expected margin.

5. Understand Shipping Options

The main international freight options include air freight, sea freight and courier services.

Air Freight

Air freight is generally useful when speed is important or when goods are relatively small, lightweight or valuable. It may be more expensive per unit of weight than sea freight.

Sea Freight

Sea freight is commonly used for larger, heavier or bulk shipments. It may offer lower transportation costs for some shipments, but transit times, handling charges and arrival-related costs must be considered.

Courier Services

Courier services may be convenient for samples, documents and smaller parcels. Confirm what the quoted service includes, including delivery, clearance arrangements and any additional charges.

Always compare quotations based on what is included, not just the headline freight price.

6. Prepare for Customs Clearance in Kenya

Imported goods may require documentation, customs declarations, payment of applicable taxes and compliance with product-specific requirements before release. Requirements can differ depending on the commodity, origin, intended use and applicable regulations.

Documents commonly associated with import transactions may include:

  • Commercial invoice
  • Packing list
  • Bill of lading or air waybill
  • Import permits or approvals, where required
  • Certificates of origin, where relevant
  • Product conformity or inspection documentation, where applicable
  • Customs entry and supporting documents

Do not assume every product uses the same documents. Confirm the applicable requirements before shipping, and consult a licensed customs clearing agent or the relevant Kenyan authorities when necessary.

7. Calculate Your Selling Price and Profit

After estimating the total landed cost, calculate the price at which you can sell your goods. Remember to include local business costs such as packaging, rent, delivery, payment fees, marketing and losses from damaged or unsold stock.

Simple profit calculation:

Sales Revenue − Total Costs = Profit

For example, if your total cost of obtaining and selling a batch of products is KSh 80,000 and you sell the batch for KSh 110,000, your estimated profit before any other unaccounted costs is KSh 30,000.

This is an illustration only. Use your actual costs, quantities and selling prices when making business decisions.

8. Common Beginner Mistakes

  • Ordering goods before checking demand.
  • Trusting a supplier without independent verification.
  • Calculating profit using the purchase price only.
  • Ignoring taxes, shipping and clearance expenses.
  • Failing to confirm import restrictions or permits.
  • Using all available capital on inventory.
  • Not keeping invoices, payment records and shipping documents.
  • Failing to plan for delays, damaged goods or slow sales.

9. Checklist Before Placing an Import Order

  • ☐ I have identified potential customers.
  • ☐ I have researched local selling prices.
  • ☐ I have verified my supplier.
  • ☐ I have confirmed the product specifications.
  • ☐ I have obtained a shipping quotation.
  • ☐ I have estimated all landed costs.
  • ☐ I have checked applicable permits and regulations.
  • ☐ I have calculated the expected profit margin.
  • ☐ I have a plan for selling and storing the goods.
  • ☐ I have enough funds to manage unexpected expenses.

10. Start Small and Keep Records

International trade can be rewarding, but it also carries financial and operational risks. New traders should learn the process, research carefully and avoid committing more capital than they can afford to risk.

Maintain records of supplier quotations, payments, invoices, freight costs, clearance expenses, inventory and sales. Good records help you identify profitable products, understand your actual costs and make better decisions when planning future orders.

MosesDigital Tip: Before you order, calculate your landed cost. A low supplier price does not automatically mean a profitable import. Know your total cost before deciding whether to buy.

Use this guide as a starting point for research. Customs requirements, taxes, permits and procedures can change. Verify current requirements with the relevant authorities and qualified professionals before proceeding with a shipment.
